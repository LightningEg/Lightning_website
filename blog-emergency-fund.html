Emergency Fund in EGP: How Much You Need and How to Start

An emergency fund is for essential, unexpected costs: lost income, medical care or an urgent repair. Its size comes from your household’s needs, not from one number for everyone.

Part of Budget Your Salary · Reading time: 5 minutes · Last updated: 2026-10-23

An emergency fund lowers the chance that you have to borrow or sell something at a bad moment when a problem hits. To set its target, don’t start from your salary. Start from what it costs to cover the essentials if your income stops for a while.

1. Work Out Your Essential Spending for a Month

Add up what is hard to stop: housing, basic food, electricity, gas, water, internet and phone, the transport you need for work, medicine, and any instalment that can’t be postponed. Leave out going out, optional purchases and saving for ordinary goals.

2. Choose the Number of Months for Your Situation

3 to 6 months is a common range, but it is not a fixed recipe. An employee on a steady salary in a household with more than one income is in a different position from someone who does seasonal work or supports parents. Ask yourself: if my income drops, how long would I need to find another source? Do I have health cover through work, or someone to fall back on? Do I have large monthly commitments? If losing income could last longer, raise the target.

If the big target feels far away, start with a nearer milestone: an amount that covers an urgent bill or two weeks of essentials, then raise it in stages. A small start is better than waiting until you can gather a large figure.

3. Turn It Into a Monthly Plan

Subtract what you have now from the target, and divide the rest over a period that suits your income. For example, if the first-stage target is 12,000 EGP and you already have 3,000, you need 9,000 more. If you save 750 EGP a month, you will get there in 12 months, not counting any return or fees.

If 750 EGP a month squeezes your essentials, lower the amount or stretch the time. Move a fixed amount right after you are paid, or add part of any one-off income. But don’t build the plan on a bonus or incentive that isn’t guaranteed.

4. Keep It Reachable and Set Aside for Emergencies

Easy access matters more than a higher return. Compare the withdrawal terms, the fees and any risks of the place where you keep the money. Don’t treat gold, a share or a locked certificate like ready cash: the gold price changes every day, and there are making charges and a gap between the buying and selling price. Breaking a certificate early can cost part of the return, depending on the bank’s terms. And a share may be priced low just when you need it.

A gameya is not an emergency fund, because you are paid on a fixed turn, not when you need it. The fund doesn’t have to sit in a separate account, but record its balance as a goal so it doesn’t blend into what you can spend.

Be precise about what counts as an emergency. Urgent medical care or a sudden essential repair is an emergency; a seasonal sale or a wedding gift with a known date is a planned cost. When you do use the fund, write down why and how you will put the money back.

5. Review the Target Once or Twice a Year

If your rent goes up, the size of your household changes or your income changes, work out the essentials again. Prices change too, so a figure that was enough this year may be small next year. If you start work with irregular income, you may need a bigger reserve.

Track your progress in a spreadsheet or an app, and keep the savings goal separate from the balance you can spend today. Read also how to plan with irregular income and how to build a monthly budget.

The Short Version

Work out the reserve from your essential spending, choose a period that matches how quickly you could get back to earning, and build it step by step. The aim is realistic security you can keep up, not a number that weighs on you every month.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to reach a 6-month emergency fund straight away?

No. Six months is not a requirement, and it isn’t an amount to collect quickly. Choose a target that suits your situation and start with a small balance you can build without neglecting your commitments.

Where should I keep my emergency fund?

Somewhere easy to reach and clearly liquid, with the fees, risks and withdrawal terms in mind. Don’t assume the price of any asset is fixed, or that you will be able to sell it on the same day.

Can gold or dollars be part of an emergency fund?

You can keep some of it that way, but their prices move, and they may be worth less than you expected when you need them. Your spending is in pounds, so keep the part you will rely on for essentials easy to withdraw and of known value.

How much should I start with if my salary is small?

Start with an amount you can keep up, even 200 EGP a month, and add to it when things allow. Being regular matters more than the size of the amount at the start.
