Salary and Spending

Why Your Salary Runs Out Before the Month Ends

If your balance falls before payday, the reason is not always a small salary or careless spending. Often there are costs that repeat and that you never see all in one place.

Reading time: 5 minutes · Last updated: 2026-10-12

The Quick AnswerA salary usually runs out early because part of the spending is out of sight: commitments that are deducted automatically, small costs that repeat, money split between the bank, cash and a wallet, and costs that arrive in seasons such as school and Eid. Put a whole month in one place and you will see the gap.

Your salary arrives on the 25th, and two weeks later you work it out and find that what is left won’t last until the next payday. You ask yourself: where did I spend it? To find out, you have to look at the whole month at once: what was deducted automatically, what was paid in cash, what was moved to a wallet, and what repeats every so often.

1. Commitments First, Before Daily Spending

Rent or an instalment, internet and phone, electricity, gas and water, transport and essential household costs all take their share before any other decision. If they add up to more than you worked out, you start the month short even if you buy nothing big.

Write down each commitment with its date beside it. On payday the balance looks large, but part of it is already claimed by a bill coming at the end of the week. What is left after commitments is what is available to spend, and it is the number worth building on. To work it out quickly, see how much I can spend before payday.

2. Small Costs Add Up

A coffee, a delivery order, a ride-hailing trip. Each one on its own is a small figure. But if you spend 90 EGP a day on scattered things for 20 days, the total is 1,800 EGP. This figure is for illustration, not a forecast.

That doesn’t mean every small cost is a mistake. Ask yourself one question: if I saw the total as a single amount at the end of the month, would I choose it?

3. Your Money Is in More Than One Place

A bank statement alone doesn’t tell the story of the month. If you pay in cash, or from Vodafone Cash, or through InstaPay, or with another card, some spending won’t appear in any one account. Make a list of every place your money sits and every way you pay, and record each cost once, when it first leaves.

Moving money from your account to your wallet is not spending. If you count the bank deduction and then the purchase from the wallet, you have counted the same amount twice.

4. Costs That Come in Seasons

School fees, private lessons, car maintenance, medical care, wedding gifts, Ramadan and Eid. They don’t come every month, so they feel like a surprise, though they are known from the start of the year.

Go back over the last 12 months and write down what you remember, then divide each cost across the months of the year. A yearly cost of 6,000 EGP means setting aside 500 EGP a month. The figure is for planning only.

5. Gameyas, Instalments and Loans

A gameya is a monthly payment you make knowing you will collect when your turn comes. But until your turn comes, it is money that goes out every month. The same goes for phone and car instalments, and any advance from work or from a family member.

Write down the monthly amount of each one and its last month. If some instalments will end soon, know exactly when, because what they free up returns to what you can spend.

6. Review Your Month in Half an Hour

  1. Gather a full month of activity from the bank, the wallets and cash.
  2. Sort it into four groups: commitments, variable needs, personal choices and savings.
  3. Separate transfers between your own accounts from purchases, and mark any amounts that were refunded.
  4. Pick one thing to change next month, and give it an amount or a number of times.
  5. After two weeks, check whether the plan is on track. If it isn’t, adjust it. That is an adjustment, not a failure.

Gathering the activity from more than one place is the hardest step for many people. You can do it in a simple spreadsheet, or in an app that lets you review each transaction before you record it. Read also how to follow your money across the bank, cash and wallets and how to build a budget for your salary.

The Short Version

A salary runs out early when the picture is incomplete: a way of paying that wasn’t counted, an instalment with a different date, or a yearly cost that arrived all at once. Start with one full month in one place, then change one thing you can keep up.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my salary run out when I don’t buy anything big?

Because small repeated spending adds up, and commitments are deducted without you noticing. Put a whole month together and you will see that the total is larger than you imagined.

Is splitting my salary by percentages the answer?

Percentages are only a starting point, not a rule. Start from your real costs and the date of each commitment, then see what you can change.

Should I cut all my small costs?

No. Add them up first, and decide which ones you don’t care about. Cutting one line you can stick to is easier than banning everything.

How often should I review my spending?

Once a week; five minutes is enough. And at the end of each month, do a bigger review before you start the new month.
