How to Split a 20,000 EGP Salary in Egypt

No single split fits every household. This is a worked example that shows where to start; then change it to your own numbers.

Part of Budget Your Salary · Reading time: 5 minutes · Last updated: 2026-10-23

How should I split a 20,000 EGP salary?A quick example: 7,000 for housing and bills, 3,500 for food, 1,500 for transport, 1,500 for instalments, 2,000 for savings, 2,500 for personal spending and 2,000 for irregular costs. The figures are for illustration, not a forecast, and they change with where you live and how many people you support.

If your salary is 20,000 EGP, the question is not only “how much should I give to food?” The first questions are what has to be paid, what changes from month to month, and what you are saving for. The split below is an example for illustration only. Rent and prices differ a lot between Cairo and Assiut, and from one household to the next.

Notice that savings and irregular costs take 4,000 EGP between them, a fifth of the salary.

If housing and bills are 9,000 instead of 7,000, the extra 2,000 has to come from somewhere. Take it from the lower-priority lines, so the total stays 20,000:

If you have no instalments, don’t leave that line empty. Point it at a goal or a safety margin.

2. Start With the Fixed Costs

Write down the rent, the instalments, the subscriptions that matter and the bills with known dates, and put each one’s due date beside it. That sets their money aside before you spend from the salary.

Then estimate food and transport from the last two or three months, not from a figure you heard from someone whose life is different from yours.

3. Make Savings a Clear Line

If your goal is an emergency fund, a car instalment or school fees, write the goal’s name, the amount you want to reach and roughly when you will reach it. If 2,000 EGP is too much this month, start with less. A small amount you keep up is better than a big number you drop after a month.

A gameya (a rotating savings circle) can work as saving if it helps you stick to it. But it is not an emergency fund, because you can’t take your money out when you need it. Read how to work out an emergency fund.

Keep savings separate from everyday spending in your plan, even if the money stays in the same account. That way you remember that the balance in front of you is not all available to spend. If your goal needs the money out of easy reach, move it to another account for real.

4. Split the Variable Money by Week

After rent, bills and savings, look at what is left for food, transport and going out. If you set aside 3,500 EGP for food, that is about 875 EGP a week when split over 4 weeks.

Check it every week instead of finding out at the end of the month that it has run out. Weekly splitting is a way to keep track; it doesn’t mean every week has to look the same.

5. Review After Two Weeks

  1. 1Compare what you planned with what you actually spent.
  2. 2Write down the costs you forgot, such as a doctor’s visit or a repair.
  3. 3If an essential line has grown, change the plan before you cut something important.
  4. 4Pick one change for next month, such as fewer delivery orders or cancelling a subscription you don’t use.

A budget is a map of your money, not a verdict on your choices. You can also try a way to see your monthly spending. And if you want to build a budget from what you actually spent, see the salary budgeting guide.

The Short Version

Start with your real commitments, use the example only as a starting point, and keep the plan easy to change. A good budget shows you your choices before you spend, and lets you revisit them when things change.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this split right for everyone on 20,000 EGP?

No. These are illustrative figures. Housing, the number of people in your household, debts and the city you live in all change the budget. Use it as a model for the arithmetic and adjust the amounts to your own situation.

How much should I save from a 20,000 EGP salary?

There is no single amount that suits everyone. Start with an amount you can keep up after your essential commitments, even if it is small, and add to it when your income and spending allow.

Is there a ready-made ratio such as 50/30/20?

Those ratios are a useful starting point. But if essentials take more than half of your income, start from your actual figures instead of forcing yourself into a ready-made ratio.

I have many instalments and a 20,000 EGP salary. What should I do?

List the instalments by the date each one ends, and know when that is. Move the money that frees up to essentials or savings, and don’t take on a new commitment before one of the instalments ends.
